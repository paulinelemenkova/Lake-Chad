% Abstract (Do not insert blank lines, i.e. \\) 
\abstract{Lake Chad situated in the semi-arid region of African Sahel plays a vital role in hydrogeological balance of regional ecosystems, presents an essential water source for millions of people living around its shorelines, and provides a habitat for wildlife species including rare and migrating waterbirds. However, the lake has shrunk significantly since the 1960s, and continued to reduce in size and extent during recent decades. Trends in drying and shrinking of wetlands in Lake Chad area are caused by diverse environmental factors and changed climate patterns. The desiccation of the lake is threatening both people's wellbeing and environmental sustainability. This study focused on identification of changes in the Chad Lake water surface areas, wetland extend and associated land cover types. The methods include the use of the GRASS GIS 'i.maxlik' and 'i.cluster' modules for remote sensing data classification using three multispectral Landsat 8-9 OLI/TIRS images. The maximum likelihood discriminant analysis classifier was applied for image processing and analysis. Three satellite images on 2013, 2017 and 2022 were processed to visualise changes in the land cover types which reflect variations in water balance, wetland area and extent around the Lake Chad over the recent decade. The results demonstrate that scripting tools of the GRASS GIS provide an efficient method of digital image processing for monitoring land cover changes in endorheic lakes of Central Africa. GRASS GIS methods shown by this study provide an opportunity to better integrate Earth observation data in environmental monitoring by allowing automation of the cartographic workflow through scripts.}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Background}
Many environmental phenomena which occur in landscapes, agricultural and ecological structures present heterogeneous properties which vary in space and time at various scales \cite{Pengetal2020,Fahrig,WILSON2017218}. Various approaches of modelling, visualisation and mapping these structure are developed with aim at describing and analysing their origin, trends and nature. The principal approach of using the remote sensing data consists in classification of the satellite images using the spectral difference in the pixels on the images that represent diverse objects on the Earth's surface visible from space \cite{WEISS2020111402,Xieetal2008,ABDI2021108098}. Such fundamental properties of the remotely sensed data enables to classify the satellite images into various classes of land cover types. Comparison of several images captured on the same area for various time periods enables to visualise and analyse the dynamics in land cover types and landscape changes visible from the space borne Earth Observation data such as Landsat Operational Land Imager Thermal Infrared Sensor (Landsat OLI/TIRS) images.

In this paper, we address the problem of tracking changes in the landscapes of the Chad Lake using Earth Observation data and scripts of the GRASS GIS. The major problem is related to the dryness of the lake and changes in its present shape due to the climate changes (droughts and decrease in precipitation) and human-induced activities. Monitoring Lake Chad has a long tradition, going back many years for application of hydrological surveying which enables to detect the actual level of the water horizons in the lake \cite{Tilho,LHuillier,Cabot,Tubiana,01440}. These early approaches to evaluation of the environmental changes in the Lake Chad region relied on estimation of the climatic data series \cite{Riou} and hydrological models that estimate the volume balance in the lake based on the field observations \cite{bader2011modele}. 

\subsection{Problem Statement}

Accurate cartographic visualization has been a challenge in environmental science with a focus on Africa, where data are often scarce or vary significantly by sources, resolution, and spatial extent. The wide range of geographic data poses a great challenge for mapping approaches due to their different functionality \cite{STEINIGER2013136}. Therefore, selecting suitable GIS program is important for environmental mapping of Africa, since the functionality of the software may affect data analysis and limit cartographic techniques. A comprehensive survey on available free GIS \cite{LEIDIG201549,CHEN2010253}, recent trends in the development of cartography \cite{WangWu} and new directions and perspectives in GIS \cite{Lietal2022}, while this section only covers the most relevant GIS applications for environmental mapping.

The efficient solutions for satellite image processing are presented by ERDAS Imagine with applications of environmental monitoring \cite{FALAKI2020363} with many existing cases due to the functionality and availability of diverse modules aimed at raster data processing. The Global Mapper GIS, developed by the USGS, includes a variety of tools including LiDAR handling, 3D rendering, watershed delineation to process geological and environmental data. Probably the most-well known and widely distributed commercial GIS is the ArcGIS which process well both vector and raster data \cite{AlDjazouli}, supports satellite images processing \cite{SESNIE20082145}, enables hydrological catchment and basin analysis \cite{Nkiaka}, terrain DEM modelling for geospatial data analysis \cite{Kay}, spatial data visualization \cite{GHIENNE200281,Buenzli,Gachet} and many other functions. 

More examples of commercial GIS software include Geomedia GIS, developed by the Hexagon Geospatial for cadastral mapping, landscape and terrain analysis, and geomorphometric modelling. MapInfo GIS provides tools for environmental analysis, mapping and cartographic visualization. These and others proprietary GIS are examples of the commercial software on the geospatial market. However, such proprietary programs are restricted to the license availability which restricts their use to large business enterprises and commercial organisations with market license. This becomes inefficient for many research cases, such as educational workspace, private-run business or mapping for personal research needs. In such cases, the use of open source GIS becomes essential for effective geographic data processing \cite{Auda}. 

\subsection{Related Works}

The existing examples of mapping Lake Chad include the use of ArcGIS \cite{Nzango}, ILWIS GIS \cite{Onamuti} or QGIS \cite{PhamDuc} for environmental monitoring. These studies presents cases of satellite image processing by supervised classification, estimating shrinkage in the catchment area and vegetation mapping, or time series for analysis of lake extent and water supply sources. The fieldwork-based approaches to environmental monitoring of Chad region  uses hydrological parameters to estimate the net water balance in lake using modelling \cite{MAHMOOD2019104043}. Such parameters include the tributary inflow, precipitation, evaporation, infiltration, and additional data on soil moisture deficit, soil imperviousness, or infiltration rate \cite{MAHMOOD2019122}. 

Such approaches, among others, partially address the issue of climate-environmental monitoring by simultaneously exploring multiple hydrological parameters. However, they can handle only the relatively small-scale and restricted processes limited to the target hydrological variables and probabilistic modelling \cite{Guganesharajah} without their extend becoming converted to the indicators of land cover changes and visualised changes in landscapes. Besides, they often require careful hydrological parameters setting to converge and adjust to model and involve \emph{in-situ} sampling and data collection \cite{Robinson}. More advanced method of environmental monitoring includes the use of remote sensing data \cite{CRETAUX20061098,LEBLANC201187} and satellite images \cite{rs11212534,Coe,info14040249}. With this regards, the advanced software GRASS GIS achieves attention in environmental mapping owing to the variety of its functions that can be applied in environmental mapping \cite{jmse11040871}. 

As briefly reviewed above, the available GIS programs have limitations and may be efficient for selected operations, while restricted for automatic and complex spatial data analysis and visualization. This is mainly because these programs are designed for special needs of geographical data analysis, but perform poorly or none when programming scripts are required for large data processing. Another concern relates to the origin of data and materials, which may be captured from multiple sources, have diverse projections and resolution \cite{Fassoni,Bastola,atmos14020232}. The combination of these tools in one research for dividing research tasks is cumbersome and not always convenient, which raises up the question of the console-based program for spatial data analysis and mapping, which has a more flexibility in mapping process. This is achieved in GRASS GIS, owing to the embedded syntax of the programming language, used for mapping.

\subsection{Study Area}

Lake Chad presents an endorheic shallow, lake located in Central Africa. Its origin is connected to the large paleolake Chad which has varied in size over the centuries over the past 50,000 years due to the impact of climate factors \cite{DURAND198237,Servant1983}. The geographical location of the Lake Chad is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig01}. Nowadays, the lake has immense catchment area of the groundwaters from the neighbouring countries (Chad, Cameroon, Niger, and Nigeria) which exceeds the $\sim 500,000 km^2$ \cite{MAHAMATNOUR2022158152}. Since 1960s, the lake shrank drastically from about $25,000 km^2$ to its current size of about $1300 km^2$ \cite{Zhaoetal2022}. 
In contrast to the rich groundwater supply, the surficial inflow of the southern segment of the Lake Chad is supplied up to 80\% from only two rivers -- Chari and Logone (Figure \ref{fig01}) that originate in the southern highlands of Cameroon and the Central African Republic \cite{Hutchinson}. The norther segment depends on the minor inflows from the Komadugu Yobe River originating in Nigeria, and the overflows from the southern basin \cite{Fougou}.

The geological units in Chad region are formed by the processes of the Pan-African Orogeny during late Precambrian (pCm), Figure \ref{fig02}. These are the results of the crustal evolution of the area that terminated with most of the Chad granitoids being formed during the end of the Proterozoic \cite{KUSNIR1997549}. The Precambrian formations of Chad are dated by the Proterozoic Eon and include the series of sedimentary rock originating from volcanic material with significant mineral potential \cite{Wacrenier,wacrenier1958notice}. 

The agricultural activities of the Lake Chad region are also concentrated around these two river systems which play major role in the agricultural economics of the country. The surrounding geomorphology in the Chad Lake basin is not expressed which results in a very flat relief nearby the lake and its overall shallow bathymetry: the maximum depths are around 7 m, the average depths 3,40 m for the lake in general, 2,45 m in its southern segment and 4,55 m -- in its north segment \cite{Pedro}. The social-economic importance of the Lake Chad as a transboundary water resource is expressed in the maintenance of livelihoods for existence of local population such as fishing and farming  \cite{Sarch,resources6030041,Baroin}. This highlights the importance of measures on the environmental conservation of the lake and sustainable development of its resources.

In the past, Lake Chad was among the largest lakes of Africa with the extent varying between $10,000$ and $25,000 km^2$ \cite{Daveau}. However, during the last 60 years since the 1960s, the lake experienced the dramatic drying by around 90\% with a surface area being shrunk from $25000 km^2$ in 1960 to ca. $1350 km^2$, as reported in many publications \cite{Owonikoko,Rudincova,MAHAMATNOUR2021100824}. The reasons for such significant changes include both natural and anthropogenic factors. The first ones include the hydro-climatic factors and related processes, e.g., increased temperature, decreased precipitation and rainfall \cite{BUMA2016403,MAHAMATNOUR2021100824} which results in a series of droughts and desertification of the surroundings landscapes \cite{NWILO2020104190}. Thus, \cite{NWILO2020104190} reports the depleted vegetation and almost half of vegetation cover lost during three decades until 2010s in the Lake Chad Basin. As a consequence, this leads to the recession of water level in lake.

The human induced triggers contributing to the shrinking of the Lake Chad include the unsustainable agriculture with groundwaters used for irrigation \cite{Lemoalle}, the increasing demand for freshwater from the Chad Lake by local population \cite{Baroin} and the drilling of boreholes related to hydrogeologic activities on the development of large-scale irrigation system \cite{BIRKETT2000218}. The intensive modern irrigation methods and systems of farming in agriculture practising include the use of fertilizers and farm machinery for maize and millet production that also contribute to the degradation of the landscapes \cite{NILSSON201615}.

Apart from the general trends in drying, there is a regional difference between the southern and northern segments of the Chad Lake, where the northern pool is notable for a larger extent of variability and and increased vulnerability to droughts \cite{LEMOALLE2012247}. The local sensitivity to the desiccation of Lake Chad is triggered by the regional differences in the rainfall patterns and vegetation patterns in the landscapes of the Lake Chad. Thus, the remaining area of permanent open water in the southern segment of the Chad basin is surrounded by a more densely vegetated swamps and small pools which helps to maintain a lacustrine environment and prevent the southern segment from desiccation \cite{Lauwaet}.

An overall trend in dryness and desertification of sub-Saharan regions includes the expansion of the desert zone southwards, as well as disappearance of forests replaced by the savannah shrubs and grasslands. At the same time, there are reported fluctuations of the dry and wet phases in the Chad Lake region reconstructed for Quaternary period \cite{Rognon}. Those are supported by the modelled climatic oscillations that existed in central Africa as proved by the sedimentological and geomorphological analysis indicating the location of alluvial flood plains, terraces and lacustrine deltas in the past \cite{Servant1975}.

\section{Materials and Methods}

To perform image processing for Landsat 8-9 OLI/TIRS imagery, we apply the Geographic Resources Analysis Support System (GRASS) Geographic Information System (GIS) introduced by \cite{Neteler2004} using the major approach of 'i.maxlik' for unsupervised classification \cite{Neteler2008}. The embedded algorithms discriminating the pixels account for individual identities of the land cover types and objects represented on the satellite images using the information on their spectral reflectances. The map of the study are has been prepared using Generic Mapping Tools (GMT) \cite{Wessel} following the existing techniques \cite{Paul,Lemenkova202228}.

\subsection{Methods}

Programming methods and scripts present a recent trend used in geospatial data processing that emerged as a powerful alternatives to the traditional GIS software \cite{8882177,technologies11020046,7248095}. Libraries are tuned as a collection of codes specially designed to optimise specific tasks such as computing vegetation indices, projecting the data, and then concatenate the code into longer scripts which perform the complete task of mapping. Such a partition of the workflow enables the machine to perform selected tasks in satellite image processing, and thus optimises computer memory and resources. In contrast to many conventional software, the GRASS GIS supports scripts for optimization of the data processing and cartographic workflow.

Among the diverse existing methods and software that can be used for satellite image processing, scripting-based approach of GRASS GIS stands apart due to its advantages and functionality. Scripts of GRASS GIS minimise the workflow through the repeated parts of the code used for processing the data and enable the automated detection and classification of the objects on the images \cite{,Lemenkova202301,10008194}. This significantly decreases the possibility of human-included errors and misclassification of the pixels and enables to process big datasets due to significant level of automation. Besides the environmental monitoring, geophysical and geological mapping may take advantage of scripting algorithms in mapping which enable to adjust and tune many parameters in the cartographic workflow for plotting maps \cite{9964623,Lemenkova202230}.

The increase of the open spatial data arises a question of the effective tools for their processing. The most well-know tools for cartographic visualization and spatial analysis is GIS. While the range of GIS was limited by restricted functionality, modern technologies such as scripts GRASS GIS enable a wide range of techniques. Decades ago, principally new paradigms for cartographic data processing and mapping were proposed through scripting. The main idea of cartographic scripts is to automate and simplify mapping workflow to increase repeatability of workflow and precision of mapping. Such methods principally differ from the traditional GIS since scripts rely on a straightforward concept: every step in a mapping process is fully controlled by the lines of code, written according to the language syntax. In this way, hidden obstacles and bugs, often in traditional GIS, are avoided, and the complete procedure of mapping is improved. Compared to the existing GIS methods, scripting approach of the GRASS GIS can be considered as an alternative method in terms of cartographic visualization. 

Motivated by the advantages of scripts and libraries in image processing and powerful technical functionality of the GRASS GIS software in remote sensing applications, we propose the use of its libraries for Landsat 8-9 OLI/TISR satellite image classification. This approach partitions the image using the embedded algorithm of discriminating the pixels on the images that represent various land cover types in target area using their spectral reflectances. We evaluated three images covering the region of the Lake Chad on the three years: 2013, 2017 and 2022. 

\subsection{Image capture and import}

The images were captured from the original location in USGS EarthExplorer repository. The images were downloaded using the defined credentials with regard to cloudiness, extent, time of coverage of the Lake Chad, image IDs and access path to Landsat scenes. Every element in the data collection is treated as object parameters, among which the computational region was set by the 'g.region' module to match the scene of the Landsat images. The resolution of each image is moderate (30 m for multispectral bands of the Landsat OLI/TIRS sensors) with the file size of around 800-900 MB for the folder containing all necessary and auxiliary files for each scene. The image processing was performed on the MacBookAir Apple computer with chip Apple M1 which allowed to technically process large files with high amount of the required computer memory. 

Each image consisted of several tiles representing Landsat OLI/TIRS bands: multispectral visible bands, NIR, Panchromatic, Cirrus, two SWIR and two TIRS bands. Although the dimensions of the bands are identical, the resolution differs with 30 m for bands 1 to 7 and 9 (Cirrus), 15 m for panchromatic band, and 100 m for two TIRS bands. In this study, we only used the multi-spectral channels of the Landsat with resolution of 30 m. Therefore, one pixel on a image corresponds to the 30 meters on the Earth's surface in the original TIFF which determines the level of details of the vegetation patterns on the classification to this spatial dimension. The images were then read into the GRASS GIS environment using the 'r.import' modules. Each Landsat OLI/TIRS image contained 11 bands which were imported and processed individually using 'r.import' module. 

Selecting the multi-spectral bands from the original image was based on the 'r.import' module which collects raster data and reads them into a GRASS raster mapset environmentl using embedded GDAL library and reprojects the raster files into cartographic parameters defined for a mapset. For instance, the band 7 was imported using the following code: $"r.import input=/Users/polinalemenkova/grassdata/Chad/LC08\_L2SP<...>\_B7 output=L8\_2013\_07 resample=bilinear extent=region resolution=region"$ (here for 2013), and repeated likewise for relevant Landsat bands for 2013, 2018 and 2022. Alternatively, raster files can be imported using the 'r.in.gdal' library of GDAL (see the scripts) and the images were stored in the working folder using location and mapset of the current project. Once the images were read in the GRASS GIS environment, several steps of the developed framework on image processing were implemented, as described below. 

The metadata of the images were checked using relevant modules of the GRASS GIS. In particular, the techniques of image processing in GRASS GIS include the auxiliary functions of 'g.list rast' and 'r.info' acting on imported raster bands and showing the identified parameters of images as attributes and a given set of identifiers of technical specifications of the multispectral images: resolution, minimal and maximal values, coordinate system.

\subsection{Image classification}

Two major approaches in processing satellite images include the unsupervised and supervised methods of classification. The main difference between these approaches reside in the way the images are processed as data and the discrimination of pixels on the image scene. While the first approach requires human-based supervision that might be prone to errors, the unsupervised classification is a machine-based method which is more objective and free of bias. In this study, we use the unsupervised classification method using maximal likelihood classifier. The 'i.group' module of the GRASS GIS was used for processing the structures of the spatial data and grouping them into classes using the k-means algorithm. The classification was performed using the maximal likelihood approach by the 'i.maxlik' GRASS GIS module, which classifies the cells on the images using their spectral reflectance. 

The processing workflow for each image included clustering of images on each scene extracted from the original raster file, detection of clusters with centroids, assignment of pixels to these clusters based on individual spectral reflectance values and indicating their representative values by creating signature file. In such way, it detects lans cover classes for each image for a short-term sequence of images on 2013, 2018 and 2022. The land cover classes were identified dividing the image into repetitive  segments of vegetation patterns automatically using the maximum likelihood approach of the GRASS GIS. The 'i.maxlik' module of the GRASS GIS was used for automatic generation of land cover classes based on the minimal distance search algorithm that assigns pixels to the classes based on the distance between pixel's value and centroid of the tested class. In this procedure, it uses the signature file generated automatically by the 'i.cluster' module with indicated number of target classes. 

Regular intervals of the land cover classes in the Lake Chad region are represented in Figures \ref{fig05}, \ref{fig06} and \ref{fig07} showing landscape patterns for 2013, 2018 and 2022, respectively. Identifying of vegetation patterns and wetlands around the flooded area of the lake was done for target years using the detected land cover classes interpreted by the FAO classification available for Lake Chad region. Annotating the land cover classes was performed using signature file by identifying the breaks of intervals between the target classes using the difference in the spectral reflectance values of the pixels (cells on the Landsat image). Similar classes (e.g., three types of savannah) were grouped based on the original resolution of the images (30 m) which enabled to identify vegetation patches with size larger that this size. In this way, the classes were grouped and located closely to the patches of landscapes with similar spectral reflectance values in the original Landsat images. The algorithm performed automatic clustering and classification of each image in less than 2 seconds and processed raster files for the years 2013, 2018 and 2022 using the described methods.

\section{Results}

The annotating of the land cover classes around Lake Chad based on recognised image features aimed at accurate object recognition during classification of the satellite images which required preliminary examination of the images and analysis of major landscape types according to FAO classification. Hence, the following 17 classes were selected and identified, according to FAO: 1) gallery forest and riparian forests; 2) woodland; 3) swamp forest; 4) savannah (Sahelian short grass savannah and herbaceous savannah); 5) steppe; 6) agriculture and irrigated areas; 7) settlements and urban areas; 8) bare soil; 9) rocky land; 10) sandy areas; 11) water bodies; 12) wetlands and floodplain with aquatic plants. Image features and associate land cover classes were recognised in the images, identified and described as patches of land cover classes constituting the landscapes of the Lake Chad. 

Pixels or objects with similar spectral characteristics on the images were allocated to the designated classes using the  algorithm of the a maximum-likelihood discriminant analysis classifier embedded in the GRASS GIS. Using this approach, the 'i.maxlik' module enabled to classify the three images and indicate specific land cover classes around the Lake Chad. The annotations of the objects as land cover classes included the recognition of the distribution of their extent, changes from 2013 to 2022, detected shrinkage in water areas and wetlands around the Lake Chad and partially overlapped classes in the savannah regions. The polygon structure on the landscape mosaic on the classified maps demonstrates the interspersed patches of the landscape segments  which are identified on the Landsat images from the 2013 to 2022 consequently. Likewise, changes in the annotated land cover classes on the images demonstrate the climate effects which resulted in the variations of the land cover classes used for identification of the environmental changes around Lake Chad.

\section{Discussion}

Visualising the spatio-temporal changes in the borders of the Lake Chad is essential for environmental monitoring in Chad region. The basin of he Lake Chad is a subject to the challenges due to  anthropogenic impacts and variability in climate patterns. Therefore, mapping changes using satellite images on different dates enables to assess changes in the lacustrine landscapes of the Chad Lake. The anthropogenic effects may be diminished to the adaptive ecological monitoring measures, as well as sustainable agriculture and farming in Chad Lake region. Advanced approach to the visualization of the land cover types in Sahelian Africa is through cartographic processing of the remote sensing data \cite{rs12152437,Lemenkova202229,rs15030560}. Using remote sensing data in mapping workflow becomes possible due to the recent development in space borne technologies which enable to apply advanced methods for monitoring environmental changes using satellite images \cite{Bardinet1981,Bardinet1983,rs9101032,Lemenkova202227,LI2021126934}. 

We have extended the studies on the variations of the Lake Chad and land cover changes in its surroundings. To evaluate the dynamics in the landscapes of the Chad Lake, we presented the application of the GRASS GIS algorithms of libraries i.maxlik and others for processing the three satellite images Landsat 8-9 OLI/TIRS taken on three years: 2013/Nov/22, 2017/Nov/17 and 2022/Dec/09. The idea is to visualise the changes in the land cover types by comparison of the images collected on the same calendar month for different years which enables to assess the changes. The objective approach of the unsupervised machine-based classification of the images strengthens the discrimination of the image represented as triplets for the multispectral bands of the Landsat images through the maximum likelihood classification within the frame of remote sensing theory on image properties and variations in spectral reflectance. 

The proposed framework benefits from the GRASS GIS functionality and coding approach, and exhibits advantages of the Landsat OLI/TIRS scenes that balance the accuracy of visualization and complexity of the land cover types and retain sufficient discrimination threshold for image classification. Practical results are presented as maps based on the classified images. Finally, we effectively optimise the workflow of image processing and plotting using the alternative methods of scripts which are implemented in this work both in GMT and GRASS GIS, and evaluate them for mapping, annotation, and classification tasks. The GRASS GIS method is particularly efficient in terms of mapping production costs, since the toolset is free and open for use. However, although the range of technical functionality of GRASS GIS is wide, the use of scripts in cartography has certain limitations. In particular, it requires a familiarity with the programming paradigm, which supports scripts through the embedded language. 

In fact, the availability of the open source advanced cartographic programs, such as GRASS GIS, scales with the learning approach required to master the methods. In addition, the import of datasets is often performed via the integration with other libraries and Python. This article has presented a GRASS GIS script-based method for satellite image processing and cartographic visualization of land cover changes in the region of Chad, central Africa. Unlike previous traditional GIS-based methods of mapping which operate in the spatial domain, we performed plotting through the GRASS GIS syntax using codes which ensured the comprehensive workflow for the cartographic specification of the maps. The GIS-based methods relying on graphical user interface (GUI) tend to use the pre-defined designed tools and graphical methods of representation, while the proposed GRASS GIS-based method outputs maps which is made using individual adjustments in each lines of the programming code using expressions and syntax of the GRASS GIS. We have also compared the maps pairwise for various years and considered the underlying reasons for land cover changes with regard to the climate and environmental setting of the Sahelian region of Chad in central Africa.

\section{Conclusions}

As demonstrated in this study, the GRASS GIS, an advanced cartographic tool and GIS, provides a logical language with a straightforward syntax for a console-based cartographic mapping using shell scripts for image processing. As a machine-based method method, it is a fast and effective technique, compared to the conventional mapping workflow. The application of GRASS GIS shown the advantages of scripting languages as part of the new directions in modern cartographic development and remote sensing data processing. Setting the parameters of the GRASS GIS codes enables to produce the highest possible quality of maps, including matching the projection and colour palette tables (CPT).

The algorithm approach of scripts by the GRASS GIS is largely based on using its syntax, which is very similar to the programming languages. The lines of codes perform operations controlling the appearance of the cartographic elements on the maps and form a script for presented maps plotted in JPG (or other bitmap raster formats) which can be converted to other formats. Apart from the traditional JPG, GRASS GIS recognises a variety of miscellaneous grid formats, and enables data capture via GDAL (r.in.gdal). Using GRASS GIS for satellite image processing and mapping lead to a higher quality of the produced maps, and lower time required for workflow due to the reduced time required for scripting, compared to the cumbersome and time-consuming data processing in traditional menu-based GIS. Thus, in contrast to existing software, GRASS GIS presents a free set of command-line tools and options for processing of geographic data in both raster and vector formats, and in particular, for satellite image processing.

The maps were plotted by the GRASS GIS scripts using the image analysis and classification of the Landsat scenes covering the region of Chad. The demonstrated methods included automated scripting approach that ensures high print quality and accuracy of maps, prepared using the high-resolution data. Rather than using handmade cartographic workflow as in similar GIS-based studies, the GRASS GIS scripting presents improved methodology that emphasises the importance of programming as a new trend in contemporary cartography and remote sensing.A useful extension of the presented study would be to automatically generate a series of maps covering particular periods of droughts in semi-arid climate of Africa for a complete environmental risk assessment. Adding more datasets, for example, climate data, could extend the variability of factors for risk assessment.
